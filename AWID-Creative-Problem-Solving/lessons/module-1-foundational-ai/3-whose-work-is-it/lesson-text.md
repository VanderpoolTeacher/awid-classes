# Whose Work Is It?

## A story to start with

Someone snaps a nice photo of a downtown art museum. They run it through an AI tool and add
dramatic laser lights swirling around the building. It looks amazing. They post it.

And right away, there's a knot in their stomach. Because they know some people in the arts
community are going to see it and feel *robbed* — not because the lasers are fake, but because
the AI that made them learned from the work of countless artists who never agreed to it, were
never paid, and were never credited. To a painter or photographer, that doesn't feel like a fun
effect. It feels like theft.

That's the uncomfortable question this lesson sits with: AI learns by soaking up enormous
amounts of human-made work — writing, art, music, photos. That's *why* it's so good. And it's
why using it raises a real question: **whose work is it, and is using AI fair?** There's no
tidy answer yet (the law itself is still arguing it out). But by the end, you'll be able to use
AI *responsibly* — aware of the objection, clear on what's protected, and deliberate about your
sources and your intent.

---

## The objection: "you used my work without asking"

To get why people are upset, sit in a creator's chair for a second.

AI got good by training on mountains of human work — a lot of it copyrighted, almost none of it
licensed by the people who made it. So when you generate art "in the style of" a real artist,
or a song that sounds like a working songwriter, that artist can reasonably feel their life's
work was taken — and is now being used to compete against them. This isn't a fringe complaint.
It's serious, it's common, and the people who feel it might be exactly the audience you're
trying to reach.

We've seen this kind of fight before. When music suddenly became free to download, the creative
world was furious, and it took years for new rules to settle. AI is in that same messy
in-between right now.

## What the law actually protects: ideas vs. expression

Here's the part that surprises people: **you can't copyright an idea.** "A detective story set
in space" belongs to everyone. What *can* be protected is your specific **expression** — the
exact words, the exact image, the exact arrangement you made.

That cuts both ways. It's why AI can play with common ideas without breaking the law. And it's
why *your* specific, hard-won work — the exact wording of something you spent months building,
your particular design — is yours, even though the general topic isn't. The hours you put in
and the perspective only you have: that's the part that's protected.

## Fair use is a gray area — don't assume

You may have heard of "fair use" — a rule that sometimes allows limited use of protected work
for things like commentary, education, or parody. For AI, though, fair use is genuinely
**unsettled**; courts are deciding it as we speak. So treat it as a gray zone, not a free pass.

Two warning signs you're near the line:
- A tool spits out an image with a **stock-photo watermark** baked in. That's a giant red flag
  about where the training data came from — and a clear "don't use this" signal.
- You're generating something in the **exact style of a living, named artist.**

The safe path is simple: **prefer sources you're allowed to use.** Public-domain images — like
U.S. government and NASA photos — are paid for by the public and free to use. Your own photos and
writing are yours. Build from those, and the ownership question mostly disappears.

## Keep an honest trail

You don't have to stamp "made with AI" on everything you create. But it's smart to **keep a
quiet note of how you used AI** — at least for yourself. AI output always comes from somewhere,
and if anyone ever asks how you made something, that honesty trail protects you. The point isn't
to confess. It's to be accountable.

## It comes back to intent

In the end, AI is a tool. A hammer can build a house or break a window — the hammer isn't the
ethics, *you* are. Using AI to organize your own messy notes is a world away from passing off a
living artist's style as your own original work. Same tool. Very different choices.

## Wrapping up: back to the laser lights

Remember the laser-lights photo?

Nothing about the tool decided whether that post was okay — the *person* did. If those lasers go
on their own photo, shared with a crowd that just wants something fun, fine. If they imitate a
specific artist's look and get sold as original art to people who'd feel cheated, that's a
different thing entirely. Same image, same tool — the fairness lives in the choices around it.

That's the whole lesson. You can't dodge the question by blaming or trusting the tool. You make
it fair by knowing your audience, choosing sources you're allowed to use, and being honest about
how you worked.

**Remember:**
- Many creators see AI as trained on **unlicensed** work — a serious, widely held objection.
- Copyright protects **expression, not ideas.**
- **Fair use is a gray area** — prefer public-domain or owned sources.
- Keep an **attribution** trail, and remember the ethics live in **your intent**, not the tool.
