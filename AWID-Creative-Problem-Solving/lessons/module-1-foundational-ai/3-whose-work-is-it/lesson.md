# Module 1 — Whose Work Is It?

> **How to read this lesson:** Every time we introduce a piece of technology, we use the
> same three-part pattern — **What it is → What it does → How it fits into the ecosystem.**

---

## 1. Overview

Someone takes a nice photo of a downtown art museum, runs it through an AI tool, and adds
dramatic laser lights swirling around the building. It looks incredible. They post it.

And immediately there's a knot in their stomach — because they know that some people in the
arts community are going to see it and feel *robbed.* Not because the lasers are fake, but
because the AI that made them was trained on the work of countless artists who never agreed to
it, were never paid, and were never credited. To a painter or photographer, that can feel less
like a cool effect and more like *theft.*

Here's the uncomfortable truth this lesson sits with: AI learns by absorbing enormous amounts
of human-made work — writing, art, music, photos. That's *why* it's so capable, and it's also
why using it raises a real question: **whose work is it, and is using AI fair?** We won't hand
you a tidy answer (the law itself is still arguing). But you'll leave able to use AI
*responsibly* — aware of the objection, clear on what's actually protected, and deliberate
about your sources and your intent.

---

## 2. Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Explain** why many creators see AI as built on "stolen" work — the plagiarism/theft
   objection. *(Understand)*
2. **Distinguish** what copyright protects — general *ideas* versus your specific *expression*.
   *(Analyze)*
3. **Apply** responsible-use habits: document your AI use and prefer public-domain or owned
   sources. *(Apply)*
4. **Evaluate** whether a particular AI use is fair and appropriate for your audience and
   intent. *(Evaluate)*

---

## 3. Key vocabulary

We use the **What → Does → Fits** pattern for the main ideas, then a quick table for the rest.

### Intellectual property (IP)
- **What it is:** the legal idea that creations of the mind — writing, art, music, designs —
  can be owned.
- **What it does:** gives creators some control over how their work is copied and used.
- **How it fits:** it's the framework the whole "is AI theft?" argument plays out inside — and
  the reason your audience may care a lot about how you made something.

### Copyright
- **What it is:** the specific protection that covers a *fixed expression* of an idea.
- **What it does:** protects the particular way you said/made something — your exact words, your
  exact image — not the underlying idea.
- **How it fits:** it's why "I had an idea for a story about a wizard" isn't protected, but a
  specific written story is. The line between idea and expression is the whole game.

### AI as a tool
- **What it is:** a general-purpose tool, like a hammer or a camera.
- **What it does:** whatever you point it at — build something useful, or do harm.
- **How it fits:** the ethics live in *your intent and choices*, not in the tool itself. A
  hammer can build a house or break a window.

| Term | What it is | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Public domain** | Work free for anyone to use (e.g., most U.S. government images) | The safe source — NASA photos, public records |
| **Fair use** | A gray-area legal allowance for limited use of protected work | Unsettled for AI; don't assume it covers you |
| **Attribution** | Noting where something came from / that AI was used | Your honesty trail if you're ever questioned |
| **Plagiarism** | Passing off others' work as your own | The accusation the theft objection is really about |

## 4. The lesson

### The objection: "you're using my work without asking"
To understand the anger, see it from a creator's chair. AI got good by training on mountains of
human work — much of it copyrighted, none of it licensed by the people who made it. So when you
generate art "in the style of" someone, or a song that rhymes like a real songwriter, a working
artist can reasonably feel that their life's work was taken to compete against them. This isn't
fringe — it's a serious, widely held objection, and the people who hold it may well be *your*
audience.

We've seen this movie before. When music suddenly became free to download overnight, the
creative industry was furious; eventually the law and the market sorted out new rules. AI is in
that same messy in-between right now.

### What the law actually protects: ideas vs. expression
Here's the part that surprises people: **you can't copyright an idea.** "A detective story set
in space" is free for anyone. What *can* be protected is your specific **expression** — the
exact words, images, and arrangement you created.

That cuts both ways. It's why AI can riff on common ideas without breaking the law. And it's
why *your* specific, hard-won work — the exact wording of a lesson you spent months building,
your particular design — is yours, even though the general topic isn't. The hours you put in,
the perspective only you have: that's the protectable part.

### Fair use is a gray area — don't assume
"Fair use" sometimes allows limited use of protected work (commentary, education, parody). But
for AI it is genuinely **unsettled** — courts are deciding it right now. Treat it as a gray
zone, not a free pass. Signs you're near the line: a tool reproducing a watermark (it has
happened — a generated image showing a stock-photo watermark is a giant red flag about where
the training data came from), or generating something "in the exact style of" a living,
identifiable artist.

The safe path: **prefer public-domain or owned sources.** U.S. government images (NASA, public
records) are paid for by the public and free to use. Your own photos and writing are yours.
When you build from sources you're allowed to use, the ownership question mostly disappears.

### Attribution: keep an honest trail
You don't have to slap "made with AI" on everything. But **document your AI use** — at least in
your own notes. AI output always comes from *somewhere*; if you're ever asked how you made
something, a quiet honesty trail protects you. The goal isn't confession, it's
*accountability.*

### It comes back to intent
AI is a tool. A hammer builds a house or breaks a window — the tool isn't the ethics, *you*
are. Using AI to organize your own messy notes is worlds apart from passing off a living
artist's style as your own original work. Same tool, very different choices.

## 5. Worked example

- **The laser-lights photo.** A real, useful effect — and a real risk of offending the creative
  community whose work trained the tool. The point: know your audience before you post.
- **The stock-photo watermark.** A generated image comes back with a faint watermark baked in —
  proof of where the training data came from, and a clear "do not use this commercially" signal.
- **Public-domain build.** Making a space-themed project from NASA images: free, safe, and
  nobody's been wronged — because the sources were fair to use.

## 6. Hands-on activity

> **Goal:** practice judging fairness and sourcing — not to reach one "right" answer, but to
> *reason* about it. (Process over product.)

1. Ask an image generator for a picture "in the style of [a famous living artist]." Then ask it
   for the same scene with **no artist named**. Compare. **Write down:** which one would you feel
   okay publishing, and why?
2. Find one **public-domain** image source you could actually use (search "NASA images" or
   "[your city] public records"). Note it down — that's a safe well to draw from.
3. Think of something you've made (a post, a doc, a design). **Write one line** documenting how
   AI was (or wasn't) involved — your honesty trail.

**Reflection (one sentence):** *"Using AI feels fair to me when ____, and unfair when ____."*

---

## 7. Common pitfalls (the "tells")

- **Assuming "fair use" covers you.** It's unsettled for AI — don't lean on it.
- **Ignoring your audience.** The same image delights one crowd and offends another; know which
  room you're in.
- **Copying a living artist's signature style** and calling it original. Legally murky, and a
  fast way to lose trust.
- **Confusing idea with expression.** You can borrow a topic freely; lifting someone's specific
  work is different.

---

## 8. Discussion questions

1. Is using AI trained on artists' work without consent "theft," "fair game," or something in
   between? Where do *you* draw the line?
2. You can't copyright an idea, only an expression. Does that feel right for the AI era — or does
   it need to change?
3. When (if ever) should you disclose that you used AI? Who deserves to know?
4. "The tool isn't the ethics, you are." Where have you seen the same tool used both well and
   badly?

---

## 9. Check for understanding

1. In one sentence, what is the plagiarism/theft objection to AI?
2. What's the difference between an **idea** and an **expression** — and which one can copyright
   protect?
3. Why shouldn't you assume **fair use** covers your AI project?
4. Name one **safe** source of images you can use without an ownership problem.
5. Give an example of an ethical AI use and an unethical one that use the *same* tool.

*(Answer key is in the instructor notes below.)*

## 10. Key takeaways

- Recall the laser-lights photo: a cool effect that some creators would see as built on their
  stolen work. **Know your audience and your sources.**
- Many creators see AI as trained on **unlicensed** work — a serious, widely held objection.
- Copyright protects **expression, not ideas**; **fair use is a gray area** — prefer
  public-domain or owned sources.
- Keep an **attribution** trail, and remember the ethics live in **your intent**, not the tool.

## Instructor notes

> **Framing:** This is the values-and-judgment lesson. There is no clean legal answer yet, and
> that's the point — teach learners to *reason* about fairness, audience, and sourcing rather
> than memorize a rule. Open with the laser-lights story and let the discomfort sit.

**Pacing:** Sections 1–4 ≈ 20–25 min; activity ≈ 15 min; discussion is the heart — protect
10+ min for it.

**Teaching tips**
- This lesson lives on **discussion.** Resist giving "the answer"; surface multiple defensible
  views (the artist's, the entrepreneur's, the educator's).
- Make the **idea vs. expression** distinction concrete with a quick example (everyone can write
  a "space detective" story; only one person wrote *this* one).
- Land the **public-domain** habit as the practical, safe default.
- Keep it non-preachy: the goal is responsible, audience-aware use — not "AI is bad."

**Check-for-understanding answer key**
1. That AI was trained on creators' work without consent, payment, or credit, so using it can
   feel like profiting from stolen labor.
2. An idea is the general concept (free to all); an expression is the specific fixed form (exact
   words/image). Copyright protects the **expression**, not the idea.
3. Because fair use for AI is legally unsettled — courts are still deciding — so it's a gray
   area, not a guarantee.
4. Public-domain sources, e.g., U.S. government/NASA images, public records, or your own work.
5. Same tool, different intent: organizing your own notes (ethical) vs. passing off a living
   artist's signature style as your original creation (unethical).

**Common misconceptions to watch for**
- "If it's on the internet, it's free to use." (No — most of it is copyrighted.)
- "Fair use means I can use anything for a project." (Unsettled and narrower than people think.)
- "Using AI is automatically cheating/stealing." (It depends on intent, sourcing, and honesty.)
