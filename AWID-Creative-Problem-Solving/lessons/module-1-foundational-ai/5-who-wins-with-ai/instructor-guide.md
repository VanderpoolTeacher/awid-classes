# Instructor Guide — Who Wins With AI?

## Why this lesson exists
This is the **empowering** lesson, especially for non-technical learners. The first-computers
story reframes "I'm not technical" into "I have the expertise that actually wins." Lead with it,
and keep the tone confident-but-humble (experts win; also know your edge).

## Learning objectives
1. **Explain** why AI is a tool whose value depends on the user. *(Understand)*
2. **Distinguish** domain knowledge + intent from raw technical skill as what makes an effective
   AI user. *(Analyze)*
3. **Apply** a "get closer to the controls" habit — be specific, ask for structure. *(Apply)*
4. **Evaluate** where one's expertise is a strength and where a human expert is still needed.
   *(Evaluate)*

## Materials & setup
- A chatbot on the projector for the vague-vs-specific demo.
- The student **activity handout** printed or linked.

| Segment | Time |

## Facilitation walk-through
### 1. Open with the story
Tell the first-computers story: built for ballistics, then handed to accountants and clerks — and
those domain experts, not the engineers, are who won. Land the reframe: *you already have the
scarce ingredient.*

### 2. Teach the through-line
- **The tool doesn't supply judgment** — hammer in whose hands?
- **You bring expertise + intent** — the teacher vs. the programmer.
- **Get closer to the controls** — specificity; ask for structure.
- **Agents** — AI that acts, with a plan + approval.
- **Know your edge** — high-stakes/out-of-domain → human expert.

### 3. Live demo
Ask the chatbot to "make this better," then ask again with a specific instruction (length,
audience, what to keep). The contrast *shows* that clear intent steers the tool.

### 4. Activity
Hand out the worksheet. Have learners name their own domains out loud in Part 2 — make
"you already have the edge" personal.

### 5. Discussion
Draw out where each learner's expertise gives them an AI edge; balance with the "know your edge"
humility.

### Closing thought
"AI is the most capable tool most of us will ever hold. Whether it builds something great comes
down to the hands holding it — and the clearer and more expert those hands, the better."

## Check-for-understanding — answer key
1. AI supplies speed and options, not judgment; the person sets the goal, knows what "good" is,
   and catches errors — so outcomes depend on the user.
2. Domain expertise (knowing what to ask for and when an answer is wrong) lets the expert steer
   and verify; the technical novice can't tell good from bad in that domain.
3. Getting more command over output instead of taking defaults — e.g., precise instructions
   (length/audience/what to keep) or asking for a structured format.
4. An agent is AI that can take actions (not just answer); a good one states its plan and pauses
   for approval before doing anything important.
5. When you're outside your expertise or the stakes are high — bring in a real human expert
   rather than trusting the tool.

## Common misconceptions to watch for
- "You must be technical to win with AI." (Domain expertise + clarity matter more.)
- "Better prompts are magic words." (It's specificity and intent, not incantations.)
- "Agents make the human unnecessary." (They raise the stakes on good steering and review.)

## If you have less time
Keep the opening story, the vague-vs-specific demo, and Part 2 of the activity (use your
expertise). Those carry the lesson.
