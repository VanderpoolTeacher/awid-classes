# Your Data Isn't Private

## A story to start with

It's late, the report is due, and you've got a messy spreadsheet — customer names, emails,
little notes about each one. So you copy the whole thing, paste it into a free chatbot, and
type: *"clean this up and summarize it."* Ten seconds later — beautiful. Done.

And in those ten seconds, you may have just handed a few hundred people's private information to
a company you don't control — to be stored, maybe used to train future models, and possibly kept
**forever.** You can't pull it back.

The most dangerous click in AI isn't a download. It's **paste.**

This lesson is about one unglamorous skill that protects you, the people in your data, and your
organization: **knowing what you can safely put into AI — and what you can't.** Anything you
type into a public tool may not stay private, so the habit we build is simple: before you paste,
decide whether this is yours to share.

---

## Nothing you put online is truly private

Start with the hard truth: **anything you put on the internet may not be private, and it tends
not to disappear.** These systems copy, cache, log, and back up data because that's how they
work. A simple way to say it (the way you'd explain it to a kid): *everything you do on a
computer can be tracked, and once it's out there, you can't fully take it back.*

That's not paranoia. It's just how the machinery runs.

## So be deliberate about what you feed AI

Public AI tools are part of that same internet. The less personal information you give them, the
safer you are. Before you paste, ask one question: **is this mine to share?**

If it has other people's names, emails, phone numbers, health details, or money details — strip
it out, swap it for placeholders, or don't paste it at all. A handy move: replace real names and
numbers with stand-ins like "Customer A" and "$X," get your summary, then put the real details
back yourself, on your own computer. The AI helps, but it never sees the private parts.

## Some data is protected by law

In a lot of fields this isn't just polite — it's the law. Health information is protected by a
rule called **HIPAA.** Student records are protected by **FERPA.** Other industries have their
own. If you work in one of those spaces, you need to actually know the rules *before* you point
AI at the data. "I didn't know" won't get you off the hook.

## Free vs. paid — read the terms

Here's the part most people skip: **free tools usually reserve the right to use your data**,
often to help train future models. Paid and business versions sometimes let you turn that off —
but you only find out by reading the terms.

Rule of thumb: on a free personal account, assume anything you type could be used and kept. If
that's not okay for the data in front of you, use a properly licensed tool — or just don't use
AI for that piece.

## In a business, the rules come first

At work, the data usually isn't yours — it's the company's, and competitors must never get it.
That's why organizations set up **governance**: rules about what data exists, who can see it, and
which tools are even allowed to touch it. So the question isn't only "can AI do this?" It's "is
this data *cleared* for this tool?"

---

## Wrapping up: back to that paste

Remember the customer list and the ten-second summary?

The tool did its job. The problem was the *decision* to paste private data into a place the user
didn't control. One small habit would have changed everything: swap the names for placeholders
first, or check whether that data was cleared for that tool. The summary would have been just as
good — and nobody's information would have left the building.

That's the whole lesson. AI is wonderful for the work. Just be the person who decides, *before*
the paste, what's safe to share.

**Remember:**
- **Anything online may not be private — and it doesn't disappear.**
- Keep **personal, regulated, or confidential** data out of public tools; **anonymize** when you
  can.
- **Free usually means your data is fair game** — read the terms.
- At work, **governance decides** what's allowed; "is this cleared?" comes before "can AI do it?"
