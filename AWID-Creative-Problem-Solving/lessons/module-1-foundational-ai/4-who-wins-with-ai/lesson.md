# Module 1 — Who Wins With AI?

> **How to read this lesson:** Every time we introduce a piece of technology, we use the
> same three-part pattern — **What it is → What it does → How it fits into the ecosystem.**

---

## 1. Overview

The very first computers were built to do one thing: calculate where battleship shells would
land. Expensive, room-sized, run by specialists. Then businesses got hold of them — and here's
the surprising part. The people who got the most out of those machines weren't the engineers
who built them. They were the **accountants, the salespeople, the clerks** — people who deeply
understood a *job* and used the new tool to do it faster.

That pattern has repeated with every wave of technology, and it's repeating right now with AI.
A common worry is "I'm not technical, so AI isn't for me." This lesson argues the opposite:
**the people who win with AI are the ones who know their domain and know what they want** — and
who get close enough to the controls to steer the tool. You bring the expertise. AI brings the
speed. Let's look at how to actually be that person.

---

## 2. Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Explain** why AI is a tool whose value depends on the person using it. *(Understand)*
2. **Distinguish** what makes someone effective with AI — domain knowledge and clear intent —
   from raw technical skill. *(Analyze)*
3. **Apply** a "get closer to the controls" habit: be specific and precise to steer AI's output.
   *(Apply)*
4. **Evaluate** where your own expertise makes you a strong AI user — and where you'd still need
   a human expert. *(Evaluate)*

---

## 3. Key vocabulary

We use the **What → Does → Fits** pattern for the main ideas, then a quick table for the rest.

### AI as a tool
- **What it is:** a general-purpose tool — like a hammer, a calculator, or a power drill.
- **What it does:** amplifies whatever the person points it at; it doesn't supply the judgment.
- **How it fits:** the value isn't in the tool, it's in the hands holding it. A hammer builds a
  house or breaks a window depending on you.

### Domain expertise
- **What it is:** deep knowledge of a specific field — teaching, nursing, sales, plumbing,
  whatever you actually know.
- **What it does:** tells you what "good" looks like, what to ask for, and when an answer is
  wrong.
- **How it fits:** it's the thing AI *can't* give you, and the reason non-technical experts
  often out-perform technical novices with AI.

### Abstraction ("closer to the metal")
- **What it is:** the way tools hide their inner workings so they "just work."
- **What it does:** makes things easy — but also hides the controls, so you get whatever the
  defaults give you.
- **How it fits:** the closer you get to the actual controls (precise instructions, even asking
  for output in a structured form), the more you can steer the result instead of accepting the
  default.

| Term | What it is | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Intent** | Knowing what you actually want | The thing you must bring; AI can't guess your goal |
| **Specificity** | Precise instructions vs. vague ones | More precise in → more controlled out |
| **Agent** | AI that can take actions, not just answer | Where this is heading — it can do steps for you, with permission |

---

## 4. The lesson

### The tool doesn't supply the judgment
AI is a tool. That sounds obvious, but it changes how you should think about it. A hammer is
useless and dangerous in the wrong hands and powerful in the right ones — and the difference is
entirely the *person*. AI is the same. It will confidently do whatever you point it at, well or
badly. So the interesting question isn't "what can AI do?" It's "**what do you bring to it?**"

### What you bring: domain expertise + intent
Here's the encouraging news for non-technical people: the winners with AI are usually the ones
who **know a domain deeply and know what they want** — not the ones with the most technical
skill. A great teacher can get more useful help out of AI for a lesson than a brilliant
programmer who's never taught, because the teacher knows what "good" looks like, what to ask
for, and when the answer is off.

Just like the accountants who out-used the engineers on those first computers: your real-world
expertise is the scarce ingredient. AI supplies speed; **you** supply the judgment.

### Getting "closer to the controls"
Tools hide their inner workings so they're easy to use — that's **abstraction.** It's why you
can drive a car without understanding the engine. Convenient, but it also means you usually get
whatever the *defaults* hand you.

The skill is getting closer to the controls when you want more command:
- **Be specific.** Vague in, vague out. "Make it better" gets you a shrug; "shorten this to 100
  words for a sixth-grade reader, keep the three key points" gets you a result.
- **Ask for structure when it matters.** Telling AI to give you output in a clear, structured
  form (a list, a table, a labeled format) often produces tighter, more consistent results than
  loose prose.

You don't need to be technical to do this. You need to be **clear.**

### Where it's heading: agents
Increasingly, AI doesn't just answer — it can **act.** An *agent* can take steps for you: browse,
fill a form, complete a multi-step task — ideally telling you its plan first and pausing for
your approval before doing anything important. That's powerful, and it raises the stakes on the
same lesson: the person who steers it well (clear intent, knows the domain, checks the work) is
still the one who wins.

### Know the edge of your expertise
The flip side of "domain experts win": know where your expertise *ends*. AI plus a confident
non-expert is how bad decisions get made fast. If you're outside your domain — medical, legal,
anything high-stakes — that's exactly when you loop in a real human expert rather than trusting
the tool.

---

## 5. Worked example

- **The first computers.** Built for ballistics, then handed to accountants and salespeople —
  and the *domain experts*, not the engineers, are who got the value. The pattern still holds.
- **Specific beats vague.** "Make this better" vs. "cut this to 100 words, sixth-grade level,
  keep the three key points" — same tool, wildly different results.
- **An agent ordering lunch.** AI that takes real actions (browse, choose, add to cart) — useful,
  and a reminder that the human sets the intent and approves the steps.

---

## 6. Hands-on activity

> **Goal:** feel how *your* clarity and expertise steer AI. (Process over product — notice the
> difference your input makes.)

1. **Vague vs. specific.** Ask a chatbot to "make this paragraph better" (paste any paragraph).
   Then ask again with a *specific* instruction (length, audience, what to keep). **Write down:**
   which result was more useful, and why?
2. **Use your expertise.** Pick something you genuinely know well. Ask AI to help with it — and
   then **catch one thing it got wrong or too generic.** Your domain knowledge is what caught it.
3. **Find your edge.** Name one area where you would *not* trust AI without a human expert, and
   why.

**Reflection (one sentence):** *"My domain expertise makes me a strong AI user when ____."*

---

## 7. Common pitfalls (the "tells")

- **"I'm not technical, so AI isn't for me."** Backwards — domain knowledge matters more than
  technical skill.
- **Vague prompting.** Expecting great output from "make it better" — be specific to steer it.
- **Accepting the default.** Taking the first answer instead of getting closer to the controls.
- **Trusting it past your expertise.** Confident non-expert + confident AI = fast mistakes; bring
  in a human expert for high-stakes, out-of-domain work.

---

## 8. Discussion questions

1. The accountants out-used the engineers on the first computers. Where might *your* expertise
   give you an edge with AI that a "tech person" wouldn't have?
2. Abstraction makes tools easy but hides the controls. When is "just give me the default" fine,
   and when do you need to steer?
3. Agents can take real actions for you. What would you let one do — and where would you insist
   on approving every step?
4. How do you know when you've reached the edge of your expertise and should bring in a person?

## 9. Check for understanding

1. In one sentence, why does the *person* matter more than the tool when using AI?
2. Why can a non-technical domain expert often out-perform a technical novice with AI?
3. What does "get closer to the controls" mean in practice? Give one concrete example.
4. What is an AI **agent**, and what should a good one do before taking an important action?
5. When should you *not* trust AI on your own, and what should you do instead?

## 10. Key takeaways

- Recall the first computers: the **domain experts**, not the engineers, won — and that pattern
  is repeating with AI.
- AI is a **tool**; the value is in the hands holding it — **your domain knowledge and intent.**
- **Get closer to the controls** — be specific, ask for structure — to steer instead of
  accepting defaults.
- Know the **edge of your expertise**; high-stakes and out-of-domain work needs a human expert.

## Instructor notes

> **Framing:** This is the empowering lesson, especially for non-technical learners. The
> first-computers story reframes "I'm not technical" into "I have the expertise that actually
> wins." Lead with it.

**Pacing:** Sections 1–4 ≈ 20–25 min; activity ≈ 15 min; discussion ≈ 10 min.

**Teaching tips**
- Run the **vague-vs-specific** demo live — it's the fastest way to *show* that clear intent
  steers the tool.
- Invite learners to name their own domains out loud; make the "you already have the scarce
  ingredient" point personal.
- Hold the line on **knowing your edge** — empowerment plus humility, not overconfidence.
- Keep "agents" concrete and grounded (it takes actions, asks permission); don't drift into hype.

**Check-for-understanding answer key**
1. AI supplies speed and options but not judgment; the person sets the goal, knows what "good"
   is, and catches errors — so outcomes depend on the user.
2. Because domain expertise (knowing what to ask for and when an answer is wrong) matters more
   than technical skill; the expert can steer and verify, the novice can't.
3. Getting more command over the output instead of taking defaults — e.g., giving precise
   instructions (length, audience, what to keep) or asking for a structured format.
4. An agent is AI that can take actions (not just answer); a good one states its plan and pauses
   for your approval before doing anything important.
5. When you're outside your expertise or the stakes are high — loop in a real human expert rather
   than trusting the tool.

**Common misconceptions to watch for**
- "You need to be technical to win with AI." (Domain expertise + clarity matter more.)
- "Better prompts are about magic words." (It's about specificity and intent, not incantations.)
- "Agents make the human unnecessary." (They raise the stakes on good human steering and review.)
