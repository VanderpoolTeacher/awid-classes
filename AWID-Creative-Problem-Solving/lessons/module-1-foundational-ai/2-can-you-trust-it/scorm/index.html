Can You Trust It?

⚖️ A story to start with

A lawyer is up against a deadline. To save time, he asks an AI to summarize past court cases that back his argument. Out it comes — polished, confident, with real-sounding names, citations, and quotes. He files it.

One problem: the cases don't exist. The AI invented them, in language so professional nobody double-checked. Then the judge checks. Now his credibility — maybe his career — is on the line.

AI is a fast prediction machine: it produces text that's likely-sounding, not guaranteed-true. So can you trust it — and how do you check?

Confident does not mean correct

The trap in one line: AI is often most convincing exactly when it's wrong.

It was built to sound fluent and sure of itself. Fluency is its talent — not truth. When it's wrong, the answer doesn't come out shaky. It comes out clean, confident, and professional. That smooth confidence is what fools smart people.

Why it makes things up

It predicts the next words from patterns, over and over. When it doesn't actually "know" something, it doesn't stop and admit it — it predicts the most plausible-looking answer and hands it over.

Ask for sources and it can invent real-looking citations. Ask for case law and it can write cases that never happened. The word for this is hallucination: stating something false as if it were true. No malice — just a prediction machine filling a gap with something that fits the pattern.

The tells — and the quieter problem, bias

Tells that output may be wrong: fabricated specifics (citations/quotes you can't find), answers that fit your wish a little too perfectly, and in images — extra fingers, melted text, details that fall apart when you zoom in.

Bias is the quiet risk. Because AI learns from human data, it inherits human skew — early image tools couldn't make a balanced classroom. "Confident" doesn't mean "fair," and the unfair parts are easy to miss because nothing looks broken.

The fix: verify, and match effort to stakes

Never publish or act on AI output without checking it against a trusted source first. Do it on the back end — after the draft, before it goes out. Check the claims (do those sources exist?), and constrain the inputs (give it trusted documents, not "the whole internet").

Match your effort to the stakes. Brainstorming a bake-sale name? Relax. A legal brief, a number in a proposal, anything public with your name on it? Verify hard. Judging the stakes is the whole skill.

⚖️ Back to the lawyer

The AI wasn't malicious — it did its one job: predict text that looks like a right answer. It looked so professional he skipped the back-end check, and the stakes were sky-high. One step — do these cases exist? — would have saved him.

Treat AI like a brilliant intern whose work you always proofread before your name goes on it.

Remember:

  • Confident ≠ correct — it's often most convincing when wrong.
  • Hallucination = confident, plausible-sounding fabrication.
  • Watch the tells; stay alert to bias.
  • Verify on the back end, and match effort to the stakes.
