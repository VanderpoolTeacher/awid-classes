# Instructor Guide — Whose Work Is It?

## Why this lesson exists
This is the **values-and-judgment** lesson. There is no clean legal answer for AI and ownership
yet — and that's the teaching point. The goal isn't a rule to memorize; it's the ability to
*reason* about fairness, audience, and sourcing. Open with the laser-lights story and let the
discomfort sit before resolving anything.

## Learning objectives
1. **Explain** the plagiarism/theft objection to AI. *(Understand)*
2. **Distinguish** ideas from expression and say which copyright protects. *(Analyze)*
3. **Apply** responsible-use habits — attribution + public-domain/owned sources. *(Apply)*
4. **Evaluate** whether a given AI use is fair for the audience and intent. *(Evaluate)*

## Materials & setup
- An image generator on the projector (for the style-with/without-a-name demo).
- Web access to show a public-domain source (e.g., NASA images).
- The student **activity handout** printed or linked.

| Segment | Time |

## Facilitation walk-through
### 1. Open with the story
Tell the laser-lights story. Sit in the discomfort: it's a cool effect *and* something a creator
could call theft. Pose the question — *whose work is it, and is this fair?* — and don't answer
it yet.

### 2. Teach the through-line
- **The objection** — trained on unlicensed work; the Napster parallel; your audience may hold
  this view.
- **Ideas vs. expression** — you can't copyright an idea; your specific work is protected.
- **Fair use is a gray area** — unsettled for AI; the watermark red flag; prefer public domain.
- **Attribution** — keep a quiet honesty trail.
- **Intent** — the tool isn't the ethics; you are.

### 3. Live demo
Generate an image "in the style of [a famous living artist]," then the same scene unnamed. Ask
the room: which would you feel okay publishing? Great springboard into the discussion.

### 4. Activity
Hand out the worksheet. Circulate. Part 1 (named vs. unnamed style) drives the best reasoning.

### 5. Discussion (protect the time)
This lesson lives on discussion. Surface multiple defensible views — the artist's, the
entrepreneur's, the educator's. Resist giving "the answer."

### Closing thought
"You can't make AI fair by blaming or trusting the tool. You make it fair by knowing your
audience, choosing sources you're allowed to use, and being honest about how you worked."

## Check-for-understanding — answer key
1. That AI was trained on creators' work without consent, payment, or credit, so using it can
   feel like profiting from stolen labor.
2. An idea is the general concept (free to all); an expression is the specific fixed form.
   Copyright protects the **expression**.
3. Because fair use for AI is legally unsettled — courts are still deciding — so it's a gray
   area, not a guarantee.
4. Public-domain sources (U.S. government/NASA images, public records) or your own work.
5. Same tool, different intent: organizing your own notes (ethical) vs. passing off a living
   artist's signature style as your original work (unethical).

## Common misconceptions to watch for
- "If it's on the internet, it's free to use." (Most of it is copyrighted.)
- "Fair use lets me use anything for a project." (Narrower and unsettled.)
- "Using AI is automatically stealing." (Depends on intent, sourcing, and honesty.)

## If you have less time
Keep the opening story, the named-vs-unnamed demo, and the discussion. Those carry the lesson;
the worksheet can be sent home.
