# Module 3 — Organize Your Work

> **How to read this lesson:** Every time we introduce a piece of technology, we use the
> same three-part pattern — **What it is → What it does → How it fits into the ecosystem.**

---

## 1. Overview

Open your Downloads folder. Be honest. It's probably a graveyard — hundreds of files with names
like `document(3)-FINAL-v2.pdf`, screenshots from months ago, things you'll never find again. We
all have the pile. And here's the frustrating part: the tools to stay organized have always
existed. The hard part was never the folders. It was *us.*

That changes the moment you pair organization with AI. This lesson is about setting up a simple
home for your work so you — and your AI — can actually find, trust, and build on it: **work in
project folders, give them a sensible structure, keep a short log of what you did, and keep a
sandbox where you can break things safely.** None of this is fancy. It's the quiet habit that
makes everything else in this course — templates, systems, repeatable processes — actually hold
together.

---

## 2. Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. **Explain** why organizing your work into project folders makes you (and AI) more effective.
   *(Understand)*
2. **Distinguish** a useful folder structure (information architecture) from a random pile.
   *(Analyze)*
3. **Apply** two habits: keep a project log, and keep a sandbox for safe experiments. *(Apply)*
4. **Evaluate** your own setup and decide one change that would make your work easier to find and
   trust. *(Evaluate)*

## 3. Key vocabulary

We use the **What → Does → Fits** pattern for the main ideas, then a quick table for the rest.

### Project folder
- **What it is:** one folder that holds everything for a single effort — files, notes, outputs.
- **What it does:** keeps a project's pieces together so you (and AI) work in one place.
- **How it fits:** it's the home base. AI tools work best pointed at a clear folder, not your
  whole scattered drive.

### Information architecture (IA)
- **What it is:** the *structure* of how your folders and files are organized and named.
- **What it does:** makes things findable — a logical place for everything, consistent names.
- **How it fits:** the difference between a system you can navigate and a pile you dig through;
  you can even have AI help you design and apply the structure.

### A project log
- **What it is:** a short running note of what you did and why (and, with AI, what you asked for).
- **What it does:** records how something was built and what changed over time.
- **How it fits:** it's your memory and your honesty trail — so future-you (or a teammate) can
  understand and trust the work without re-deriving it.

| Term | What it is | Where it fits |
|---|---|---|
| **Naming convention** | A consistent way to name files/folders | Makes things sortable and findable at a glance |
| **Sandbox** | A folder for safe experiments | Where you try things and break stuff without risk |
| **Findable** | Easy to locate later | The whole point of organizing |

## 4. The lesson

### The mess isn't a tools problem — it's a habit problem
We've always *had* folders. The reason digital life turns into a junk drawer isn't missing
technology; it's that staying organized is tedious human work we skip. The fix isn't more
willpower — it's a simple structure plus a helper (AI) that's happy to do the tedious part.

### Work in project folders
The first habit: give each effort its own **project folder**, and keep everything for it in one
place — drafts, notes, final files. This matters doubly with AI: tools work far better when you
point them at *one clear folder* than at your entire messy drive. A focused folder means focused
help.

### Give it an information architecture
A pile of files in one folder is still a pile. **Information architecture** is just the
structure: a logical place for each kind of thing and consistent names so you can find them. You
don't have to design it alone — this is a great job for AI: "here's my mess; propose a folder
structure and a naming convention," then have it help sort things in. Decide the structure once;
reuse it everywhere.

### Keep a project log
The second habit: a short **log** of what you did and why. When you build with AI, jot down what
you asked for and what changed over time. It sounds like overhead; it's the opposite. It's how
future-you understands the work without redoing the thinking, how a teammate picks it up, and how
you answer "wait, why did we do it this way?" three months later. *(This very course was built
with exactly such a running log.)*

### Keep a sandbox
The third habit: a **sandbox** folder — a no-stakes place to experiment, try a wild idea, or
break something on purpose. Don't run experiments inside your real, important work. Play in the
sandbox; when something works, move it into the real project. This is how you learn a tool
quickly without risking anything that matters.

### Why this is the foundation
Organization isn't busywork — it's what makes everything else in this course *possible.*
Templates live in folders. Repeatable systems run on organized inputs. AI gives better help when
your material is structured. Get this base right and the powerful stuff stops being fragile.

## 5. Worked example

- **The Downloads graveyard.** A folder of hundreds of junk-named files — point AI at it, have it
  propose a structure and sort them, and set the structure as the standard going forward.
- **AI-designed IA.** "Here's my mess — propose folders and a naming convention," then apply it.
- **A running log.** A short markdown note of what was asked and what changed — so the project
  explains itself later.

---

## 6. Hands-on activity

> **Goal:** set up the bones of an organized project. (Process over product — a simple structure
> you'll actually use beats a perfect one you won't.)

1. Pick one real effort (a class, a side project, a job task). Make it **one project folder.**
2. Ask AI: *"Propose a simple folder structure and a naming convention for a project about [X]."*
   Create the top folders.
3. Start a **log** file in it. Write two lines: what this project is, and what you did today.
4. Make a **`sandbox`** subfolder. That's where you'll try risky things from now on.

**Reflection (one sentence):** *"The one change that would make my work easiest to find is ____."*

---

## 7. Common pitfalls (the "tells")

- **Pointing AI at your whole drive.** Give it a focused project folder, not the junk drawer.
- **A pile with no structure.** One folder full of everything is still unfindable — add an IA.
- **Skipping the log.** "I'll remember why" — you won't; a two-line note saves future-you.
- **Experimenting in your real work.** Break things in the sandbox, not in the thing that matters.

## 8. Discussion questions

1. Why has digital clutter persisted even though folders have existed forever? What actually
   fixes it?
2. What belongs in a project's information architecture for *your* kind of work?
3. A log feels like overhead in the moment. When has *not* having one cost you?
4. What would you feel free to try if you had a true sandbox to break things in?

## 10. Key takeaways

- Recall the Downloads graveyard: the mess was never a tools problem — it was a habit problem,
  and AI can do the tedious part.
- **Work in project folders**; give them a sensible **information architecture.**
- **Keep a log** (your memory + honesty trail) and a **sandbox** (safe experiments).
- Organization is the **foundation** templates and repeatable systems stand on.

## Instructor notes

> **Framing:** This is the unglamorous-but-essential lesson. Make it personal — have everyone
> picture their own Downloads folder. The hook is that the mess is a *habit* problem AI can now
> help with, not a tools problem.

**Pacing:** Sections 1–4 ≈ 20–25 min; activity ≈ 15 min; discussion ≈ 10 min.

**Teaching tips**
- Do the **"ask AI to propose a folder structure"** demo live with a messy real folder.
- Sell the **log** by example — point at how a running log made a real project understandable
  later; keep it to "two lines a day," not a chore.
- Make the **sandbox** vivid: it's permission to experiment without fear.
- Tie it back to the bigger arc: organization is what makes templates and systems hold up.

**Check-for-understanding answer key**
1. A focused folder keeps a project's pieces in one place and gives AI a clear, contained context
   to work in (instead of your whole scattered drive).
2. Information architecture is the structure and naming of your folders/files; it makes things
   findable — a logical place for everything.
3. A short running note of what you did and why (and what you asked AI); it's for future-you and
   teammates, so the work is understandable and trustworthy later.
4. A sandbox is a no-stakes folder for experiments; keep experiments there so you don't break
   real, important work.
5. Any: proposing a folder structure/naming convention, sorting a messy folder, or organizing
   scattered notes.

**Common misconceptions to watch for**
- "Organizing is busywork." (It's the foundation templates and systems depend on.)
- "I'll remember why I did it." (Future-you won't — the log is cheap insurance.)
- "More folders = more organized." (Structure and consistent naming matter, not folder count.)
