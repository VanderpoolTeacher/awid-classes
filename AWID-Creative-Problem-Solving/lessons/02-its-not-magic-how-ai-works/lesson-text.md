# It's Not Magic: How AI Actually Works

## A story to start with

Picture this. You press play, and two voices start talking — warm, funny, a little nerdy. A
man and a woman, hosting what sounds exactly like a podcast. *"How do you teach an old
industrial giant to dance?"* one of them jokes. They riff back and forth about a city's
land-use plan like they've been doing it for years.

Here's the thing: **neither of them is real.** No microphones. No studio. No people. A tool
read a stack of dry documents and, a minute later, produced two AI voices chatting like old
friends. The first time you hear it, it's hard not to feel a little split — half amazed, half
spooked. It *feels* like magic.

It isn't. By the end, you'll know exactly why those two voices aren't magic at all. Let's pull
back the curtain.

---

## The old way: computers were boringly predictable

For most of computing history, a computer was a very predictable box. You put **instructions**
and **inputs** in one side, the box did its work, and the **output** came out the other
side — and it was the *same* output every single time.

Two plus two is four. Today, tomorrow, on your phone, on a giant mainframe — always four.
Arrange these letters this way and you get the same word. Water is water. That sameness has a
name: **deterministic.** Same input in, same answer out, forever.

That's the kind of computing most of us grew up with: spreadsheets, calculators, the cash
register at the store. Reliable. Repeatable. A little boring. And — importantly — *clearly not
magic.* Nobody thinks a calculator is alive.

## So what changed? Scale, not sorcery

Here's the surprise: the basic idea **didn't** change. A computer still takes inputs, does
work, and produces outputs. What exploded is the **scale** of two things:

- **Data** — the information the machine learned from. We now have a nearly endless ocean of
  it: every article, book, comment, and caption people have put online.
- **Compute** — raw processing speed. We can now chew through that ocean so fast that what
  used to take a mainframe *days* happens in the blink of an eye.

That speed is the real reason AI *feels* magical. Have you ever typed a question to a chatbot
and the full answer came back so fast it didn't seem human? That wasn't a tiny person typing
on the other end. It was an enormous amount of computing happening almost instantly.

## It's all just code

Everything you see on a screen — this page, your phone's apps, your TV, a chatbot's reply — is
generated from **code**: precise, step-by-step instructions a computer follows. AI doesn't
reach into thin air and pull out an answer. It **writes code, very fast, and shows you the
result.**

Say it with us, because we'll repeat it all course long: **it's not magic, it's code.**

Even pictures work this way. When AI makes an image, it isn't drawing line by line the way you
would with a pencil. It computes a whole field of "crazy colored pixels" and snaps them into
something you instantly recognize — a face, a city, a cat — so fast it looks impossible.
Impressive? Absolutely. Magic? No. Just code and speed.

## Why making sentences isn't as hard as it looks

Here's a humbling little truth that makes AI less spooky: **people are more predictable than
we like to admit.**

Think about language. There are only so many ways you can arrange words into a sentence that
actually makes sense. If someone says *"that's a load of bull——,"* your brain already knows
the next word. It's not a hard guess. Music is the same: there are only so many ways to arrange
notes into something you'd actually want to hear.

That's basically what a chatbot does — it's a very, very fast machine for guessing *what word
probably comes next*, over and over, based on the mountain of writing it learned from. It
doesn't need to "understand" you the way a friend does. It just needs to be really good at
"what usually comes next." Once you see it that way, those two podcast voices get a lot less
mysterious: they're predicting likely, natural-sounding sentences — fast.

## The twist: AI doesn't give the same answer twice

Now here's the big difference from your calculator. Ask AI the **exact same question twice**,
and you'll usually get **two different answers.**

That's because it isn't looking up one true result. It's predicting a *likely* one — and
"likely" wiggles a little each time. We call this **non-deterministic**: same input, possibly
different output. Generate something, then ask for the exact same thing again, and you simply
won't get it back the same way.

This matters more than it sounds. It means you **can't just re-ask and expect the same thing.**
If you ever need AI to do something the *same* way every time, you don't just cross your
fingers — you build a little system around it with checks and rules. (That's a big idea we come
back to later in the course.)

## You don't need to know the engine to drive the car

Maybe you're thinking, *"But I don't understand any of the deep math behind this."* Good news:
**you don't need to.**

You can drive a car without knowing how the engine burns fuel. You press the gas, it goes. But
here's the nuance — a driver who knows *roughly* how the engine works, and what it should sound
like when something's wrong, is a better and safer driver. Same with AI. You don't need the
math. You **do** want a simple, honest mental model:

> **It's code + data + speed, and it's non-deterministic.**

That little model is enough to start using AI well today — and, just as importantly, enough to
judge when *not* to trust it (which is the very next thing we'll dig into).

## One more reality check

It's tempting to think AI "knows everything." It doesn't. These systems are actually starting
to **run low on new text** to learn from — they've begun learning from video instead. That's a
useful reminder: AI is built from a **finite, human-made** pile of data. It's not infinite. It's
not all-knowing. It's a fast pattern-machine standing on a big — but limited — pile of
*our* words.

---

## Wrapping up: back to those two voices

Remember those two podcast hosts from the start?

Now you can explain them. There were no people and no microphones. A tool read a stack of
documents (**data**), used a huge amount of **compute** to predict natural-sounding
sentences — what word probably comes next, over and over — and turned that into **code** that
played as two voices. Run it again and you'd get a slightly different conversation, because it's
**non-deterministic.** Fast? Stunningly. Magic? Not even a little.

That's the whole game. From here on, every tool we touch in this course is some version of that
same idea. You don't have to be amazed or afraid. You can just be a smart user of a powerful
tool.

**Remember:**
- It's **not magic — it's code**, generated from huge data and fast compute.
- AI is **non-deterministic**: same prompt, different answers.
- The **speed** is what makes it feel magical; the substance is ordinary code.
- You don't need the math — a simple mental model is enough to use it wisely and judge its
  limits.
