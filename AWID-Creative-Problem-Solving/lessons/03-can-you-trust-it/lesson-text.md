# Can You Trust It?

## A story to start with

A lawyer is up against a deadline. To save time, he asks an AI to write a summary of past court
cases that back up his argument. Out it comes — polished, confident, full of real-sounding case
names, citations, and quotes. It reads beautifully. He files it.

One problem: **the cases don't exist.** The AI invented them — names, rulings, quotes, all of
it — and wrapped them in language so professional that nobody thought to double-check. Then the
judge checks. The cases aren't real. Now the lawyer's credibility, maybe his career, is on the
line.

Hold onto one idea: AI is a fast prediction machine. It produces text that's *likely-sounding*,
not guaranteed-true. It's not magic — it's code making very fast guesses about what comes next.
That's powerful. It's also exactly why it can hand you something that looks perfect and is
completely false. So this lesson asks the question that matters most in real work: **can you
trust it, and how do you check?**

---

## Confident does not mean correct

Here's the trap, in one line: **AI is often most convincing exactly when it's wrong.**

It was built to sound fluent, natural, and sure of itself. Fluency is its talent — not truth.
So when it's wrong, the answer doesn't come out shaky or hedged. It comes out clean, confident,
and professional. That smooth confidence is the very thing that fools smart people.

## Why it makes things up

Remember how it works: it predicts the next words from patterns, over and over. When it doesn't
actually "know" something, it doesn't stop and admit that. It just predicts the most
*plausible-looking* answer and hands it to you.

Ask for sources, and it can produce citations that look real. Ask for case law, and it can write
cases that never happened. This isn't lying on purpose — there's no intent behind it. It's a
prediction machine filling a gap with something that *fits the pattern* of a right answer. The
word for this is **hallucination**: stating something false as if it were true.

## The "tells"

Wrong output often leaves fingerprints. Learn to spot them:

- **Fabricated specifics** — exact-looking citations, statistics, names, or quotes you can't
  find anywhere else.
- **Too convenient** — an answer that fits exactly what you were hoping for, a little too neatly.
- **In images** — extra fingers, melted text, ears or hands that aren't quite right, details
  that fall apart when you zoom in.

You can even ask the AI to check its own work: *"Look at this and tell me everything that might
be made up or that you can't verify."* That's a useful filter — but it is **not** proof. A human
still confirms.

## Bias is the quieter problem

Hallucination is loud — a fake case jumps out once you catch it. **Bias** is quiet.

Because AI learns from piles of human-made data, it soaks up human skew along with everything
else. Early image tools were a clear example: ask for "a classroom" and everyone in it looked
the same; ask for "a diverse classroom" and it lurched the other way. The model can only mirror
the data it was fed. So "confident" doesn't mean "fair," either — and the unfair parts are easy
to miss precisely because nothing looks broken.

## The fix: verify on the back end

Here's the habit that separates people who get burned from people who don't: **never publish,
submit, or act on AI output without checking it against a trusted source first.**

Do the checking on the back end — *after* you have a draft, *before* it goes out the door. Two
practical moves:

- **Check the claims.** Do those sources actually exist? Do the numbers hold up? Click through
  and confirm.
- **Constrain the inputs.** If you give the AI a small, trusted set of documents to work from
  instead of "the whole internet," it has far less room to invent.

## Match your effort to the stakes

You don't have to verify everything to the same depth — that would be exhausting. The skill is
**judging the stakes.**

- **Low stakes** — brainstorming names for a bake sale, loosening up a rough draft. A light
  glance is fine.
- **High stakes** — a legal brief, a medical claim, a number in a funding proposal, anything
  going public with your name on it. Verify hard, every time.

Knowing which situation you're in is the whole game.

---

## Wrapping up: back to the lawyer

Remember the lawyer and his beautiful, fake cases?

Now you can see exactly what happened. The AI wasn't being malicious — it was doing its one
job: predicting text that *looks* like a right answer. It looked so professional that he skipped
the back-end check, and the stakes were sky-high. One verification step — *do these cases
exist?* — would have saved him.

That's the lesson. AI is a fast, fluent, confident guesser. Treat it like a brilliant intern
whose work you always proofread before your name goes on it.

**Remember:**
- **Confident ≠ correct** — AI is often most convincing when it's wrong.
- **Hallucination** = plausible-sounding fabrication, delivered with total confidence.
- Watch for the **tells**, and stay alert to **bias** as the quieter risk.
- **Verify on the back end** against trusted sources, and **match your effort to the stakes.**
